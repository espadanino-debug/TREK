# Choosing / improving the business model and the math

Source: "What Makes The Perfect Business (5 Things)" (3fsJFUvA6Ts) transcript, plus the arbitrage framing from uWdIgftpvBI. Deeper: "The Mathematics of Business, Explained" (A_tx40lNpf8).

All five are continuums, not yes/no. Score the user's business 1–5 on each, then improve the weakest in priority order (retention first). Don't tell someone to abandon a working business because of this list; use it to find levers. Early in a career, switching out of an industry with no retention can be smart.

## 1. Sticky (most important) – revenue retention

- **Logo retention**: % of customers still here (never >100%). Churn is structural (moved, died, business closed) or voluntary (you suck) – attack voluntary.
- **Net revenue retention** can exceed 100%: give cheaper customers a clear way to spend more. $9 → $99 tier is 11×; if 20% leave but 10% upgrade, NRR >100% and the business grows with zero new sales.
- **Churn curve (Skool data across categories):** biggest drop month 1 (20%+), next ~10% around month 3, then month 6; after month 6, churn falls to about 2%/mo. So: nail the first 30 days, have a reason to pass month 3, walk them to month 6.
- **Company A vs. B:** A sells 100/200/300 and loses them all each year; B sells 100/yr and keeps them. Same revenue, but A acquired twice as many customers, and marginal acquisition cost rises (often 2–3×), so B is far more profitable and calmer to own. Retention unlocks compounding.
- Sticky: term life, alarms, internet, banking, community/consumables. Not sticky: one-off education, roofing, car sales (add continuity, see money-models).

## 2. Expensive – high gross margin

Pays people better, faster cash conversion, more reinvestment. $100M at 10% margin = $20M at 50% margin in profit, with 5× less work. Low: grocery, farming, restaurants (commodities). High: media, information/education, community, data, software, pharma, "lotions and potions".

## 3. Expanding market

Ride a tailwind, or at least avoid a headwind. Shrinking: newspapers, formal education, tobacco, alcohol, brick-and-mortar retail, clerical work. Growing: energy, AI, healthcare, cybersecurity, e-commerce, alternative education (>20% CAGR).

## 4. "Air" – low operational complexity and low capex

Few variables to manage per added unit of output (podcast ad read vs. 100-restaurant chain). Low capex means less dilution and self-funded growth. Raise outside capital only when market share really matters (network effects) and ROIC is high; if you can't raise money, usually the deal economics are the problem ("$100K in, $300K back in year one" raises itself).

## 5. Unique – a moat

Low barriers to entry mean lots of competitors and falling prices (e.g. social media agencies). Moats: capital/equipment ("buy the shovel"), proprietary know-how (recipes, processes, patents: new, non-obvious, useful), and **brand** – the cheapest moat for a service: same factory belt, Revlon stamp beats generic. Coca-Cola is the reference example across all five.

## Unit economics to always compute

- Gross profit per customer (first 30 days, and lifetime) vs. CAC → LTV:CAC.
- 30-day cash test: does one customer's 30-day gross profit cover acquiring and serving two more?
- $ per action in the primary channel (per door, per DM, per call).
- Price-tier mix: what % on the premium tier and its share of profit.
